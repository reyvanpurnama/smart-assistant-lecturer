Abstract

1

Automated Writing Evaluation (AWE) tools have proved beneficial
to writing development. Research on AWE methods is essential
for improving tool performance and further comparative studies
are needed as new methods emerge. This study examines the performance of several AWE approaches, comparing rule-based and
statistical methods, machine learning (ML) models, and a large language model (LLM). These three AWE methods were applied to a
representative sample of academic essays from the TOEFL11 dataset
to compare their assessment performance. Results show that the selected LLM, GPT-4, outperformed the other two approaches in terms
of QWK and Pearson’s correlation coefficient, while the Support
Vector Machine (SVM) model in the ML approach had the highest
accuracy and the lowest mean absolute error. This paper provides a
detailed comparison of these three approaches and discusses implications for educational practice and future research around AWE.

Writing is a fundamental skill in both academic and professional
settings, yet it poses significant challenges to students and professionals at different levels. Despite a massive amount of research on
writing and its instruction, the complex nature of writing makes it
a difficult skill to teach and assess, requiring substantial time and
resources that may not be readily available in many educational
settings. The development of Automated Writing Evaluation (AWE)
tools is thus considered beneficial. AWE tools provide immediate
feedback to writers while also alleviating the assessment burden on
instructors. Some studies have recorded the positive impact of AWE
tools on the quality of writing [7, 13, 23] as well as citing increases
in writer self-efficacy and motivation [20, 21]. The value of these
tools lies not only in their efficiency but also in the ability to provide
consistent and objective feedback, a challenge often encountered
in traditional assessments.
Developments in AWE technology have evolved significantly
over the past few decades [9]. To date, there have been three main
AWE approaches, namely, rule-based methods, machine learning
(ML) models and large language models (LLMs). The earliest developed approaches are predominantly rule and statistics-based,
relying on fixed sets of predefined rules, heuristics and indices
to identify writing errors and suggest corrections [1]. These are
somewhat limited by the rigidity of their rules and may not work
well with nuances and variability that are inherent in different
types of writing. Later developments with ML have prompted researchers and developers to adopt ML models in AWE system design
[12], as these can offer more flexibility and accuracy through algorithms that learn from large datasets of annotated texts. Most
recently developed are the LLMs, such as GPT-4. These models
can evaluate writing based on vast amounts of data and complex neural network architectures, further enhancing AWE systems. The three approaches listed above each have their strengths
and weaknesses in evaluating different writing and linguistic
features.
It is therefore of interest to understand and compare the performance of these different approaches. Although some studies have
compared the performance of different AWE approaches and addressed limitations for future development [8, 16], the performance
of newer models remains under-researched. Such comparative analyses are essential not only for pedagogical purposes but also practical applications, as they help to determine the most suitable AWE
methods for assessing different aspects of writing. This study aims
to evaluate the effectiveness of traditional rule-based, statistical

2 Related works
2.1 Traditional approaches to AWE (Rule-based
and ML approaches)
As the earliest-developed method in AWE, rule-based approaches
employ a set of explicit rules and statistical techniques to evaluate
essay quality. The idea of rule-based assessment was established as
early as the 1960s [14], subsequently evolving into the Project Essay
Grade (PEG) system. The underlying assumption of this method is
that essay quality can be represented by measurable characteristics.
This involves the identification of trins, intrinsic characteristics that
human graders seek when evaluating essays. Due to the unobservable nature of trins, quantifiable measures, known as proxes, are
approximated using computer programs. For example, essay length
measured by total number of words is the prox for the trin of fluency. The emphasis on surface-level features in early AWE systems
makes them susceptible to cheating strategies (e.g. the addition of
punctuation) that can improve essay ratings without enhancing
content [4]. Other natural language processing tools were later
developed to address more sophisticated linguistic features that
could predict essay scores. Coh-Metrix is one of the comprehensive
tools that examined lexical sophistication, syntactic complexity,
and text cohesion. Crossley and McNamara [3] found that higherrated argumentative essays were characterised by higher cohesion
and coherency . Tool for the Automatic Analysis of Lexical Sophistication (TAALES 2.0) is another example, designed to assess
lexical sophistication using statistics from various corpora. Kyle et
al. [10] showed a positive relationship between lexical sophistication and proficiency in writing. Ten of their indices were able to
explain 58.0% of the variance in the actual scores. Crossley et al. [2]
also found significant negative correlation between grammatical
and mechanical errors in GAMET and writing scores by human
raters.
Another method in AWE is ML approaches, which employ statistical algorithms to learn scoring criteria without setting pre-defined
rules. ML mechanisms can be broadly classified into two main types:
unsupervised and supervised. The unsupervised mechanism does
not require the use of training data. The goal is to categorise an essay
into classes using similarity method and clustering techniques. In
contrast, the supervised mechanism utilises evaluation datasets to
train the algorithm. Its goal is to predict the score of an essay using
regression techniques. Ramesh and Sanampudi [16] categorised the
supervised ML techniques of AWE into 4 groups: regression-based
models, classification-based models (e.g. [15]), neural networks
(e.g. [17]), and the ontology-based approach (e.g. [24]). Their study
compared the prediction performance of the first three categories
of supervised ML. Poor performance of cohesion and coherence
was observed in all models when only statistical features about frequency of word occurrences were used in training. If unidirectional
semantic information was included in training, neural network
would yield better performance than regression and classification
models.

2.2

The potential of an LLM approach

LLMs are artificial intelligence models trained on vast datasets and
sophisticated algorithms. Different from the previous two methods,
LLMs have the potential to contribute to AWE given their ability to
receive prompts and respond. Some studies have begun to examine
the performance of LLMs in AWE. Mizumoto and Eguchi [12] investigated the use of the LLM GPT-3 in evaluating the ETS Corpus of
Non-Native Written English (TOEFL11) and compared these scores
to benchmark levels. Their study showed the model’s ability to
align generally well with human scoring benchmarks, though with
some score overlaps and limitations in exact score matching, as
indicated by a quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) of 0.682. Similarly,
Li et al. [11] examined the effectiveness of an upgraded version
of GPT, GPT-4, in assessing the quality of young students’ essay
revisions. It was found that while GPT-4 showed potential for evaluating revision quality, particularly with the use of detailed rubrics
outlining common revision patterns, the use of Chain-of-Thought
(CoT) prompting did not significantly enhance performance. The
initial zero-shot prompting experiment yielded an agreement rate
of 52.00% and a QWK of 0.60, indicating moderate alignment with
human raters. Subsequent experiments that included examples of
successful revisions improved the exact agreement rate to 54.50%,
but the QWK remained unchanged. Notably, GPT-4 showed higher
agreement with human scoring for Level 1 drafts, which focused
on adding evidence, compared to more complex Level 2 and Level 3
revisions that required elaboration and explanation. This suggests
that GPT-4 is better suited for straightforward evaluation tasks,
while its performance may decline when assessing multifaceted
revisions.

2.3

The present study

It can be hypothesised that different AWE approaches perform
differently when assessing writing, as indicated by the available
research [8, 16]. Nevertheless, there is a lack of research comparing the performance of traditional approaches with the latest LLM
methods. Given that LLM models are a relatively recent development, more research is warranted. This study therefore seeks to
answer one overarching question:
How does the performance of three AWE approaches, namely (1)
rule-based, (2) ML and (3) LLM, differ in predicting essay quality?

3 Methodology
3.1 Dataset
The TOEFL11 dataset was used in this study. It is a rich and wellestablished corpus of non-native written English, a type of writing
commonly used in AWE and other natural language processing
studies. The dataset is derived from the Test of English as a Foreign
Language (TOEFL), a standardised test for assessing academic English proficiency of non-native speakers. The dataset contains 12,100
essays written by test takers from 11 different native language
backgrounds from 2006 to 2007. The essays come from the TOEFL
Independent Writing Task, where test takers respond to one of
eight prompts, resulting in a variety of topics and writing styles as
well as performance levels. Each essay was first graded by trained
human raters on a 5-point scale. The sum of the scores was then
collapsed into a 3-level proficiency scale categorised into low, mid
and high groups, representing low, intermediate and high levels of

3.4

Supervised ML models

We employed a comprehensive set of natural language processing
analysis tools to capture the multifaceted nature of essay quality.
We opted for tools with relevant features to measure six common
areas of writing, namely text cohesion, paragraph organisation,
grammar usage, spelling and punctuation accuracy, language and
vocabulary choices, and syntactic complexity. The tool Coh-Metrix
was chosen for text cohesion, offering insight into how well ideas
were connected within the essays. GAMET (Grammar and Mechanics Error Tool) was used to identify grammatical and mechanical
errors. TAALES (Tool for the Automated Analysis of Lexical Sophistication) was selected to assess the sophistication of vocabulary
choice in the essays. TAASSC (Tool for the Automatic Analysis
of Syntactic Sophistication and Complexity) was used to examine
the complexity of sentence structures. These four tools collectively
extracted 322 linguistic features to measure the six aspects of writing quality listed above. This provided a rich set of data points,
allowing us to capture both surface-level features and higher-order
linguistic and textual characteristics.

For the ML approach, we used Scikit-learn Python module to evaluate two popular ML algorithms, Random Forest (RF) and Support
Vector Machine (SVM). The same 80/20 train-test split was applied
for both models to ensure a fair comparison.
In the RF approach, we first used the extremely randomized
trees (ExtraTreesClassifier function) for feature selection. The selection of the most important features helped reduce dimensionality
and possibly improve model performance. Second, we addressed
class imbalance by applying the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling
Technique (SMOTE). This data augmentation method involved the
creation of synthetic examples of the minority class, which balanced the class distribution and enhanced model performance in
later steps. Third, we performed feature scaling by standardising
the features to zero mean and unit variance using the StandardScaler function. Fourth, we applied the RandomForestClassifier
function for the actual prediction. To tune the hyperparameters, we
performed 5-fold cross-validation and selected the best parameters.
The model was re-trained on the entire training set and finally evaluated on the test set. In the SVM approach, the data transformation
procedures were similar to those of the RF, with one exception.
The difference was that the feature selection used the LinearSVC
function with L1 penalty to produce sparse solutions and eliminate
less important features.

Feature extraction

A rule-based approach

We implemented a rule-based approach to establish a baseline and
explore the predictive power of individual features. First, we stratified the sample and used an 80/20 split for the train-test ratio.
This resulted in 968 essays in the training set and 242 essays in
the test set, including 27 low-level, 131 mid-level and 84 high-level
essays. Next, we conducted a correlation analysis to identify the
features most strongly associated with essay quality. The correlation of each of the 322 linguistic features with the actual essay
levels was calculated. We sorted the features in ascending order if
the correlation sign was positive, and descending order if the sign
was negative. This allowed us to establish appropriate thresholds
for each feature for comparison. In threshold determination, we
chose the threshold for each feature based on the distribution of
low, mid, and high levels in the training data. The general rule was

values specific to each feature. As an example, consider the feature ‘number of words’ by focusing on a small sample of 7 essays

An LLM approach

For the LLM approach, we chose to evaluate the prediction performance of ChatGPT, a prominent LLM developed by OpenAI [5].
In terms of scoring, we adapted the original 5-point TOEFL rubric
into a 3-point scale (low, medium, high) to align with the scoring
provided by the given corpus. Low proficiency corresponded to
scores ranging from 1.0 to 2.0, medium to scores between 2.5 and
3.5, and high to scores between 4.0 and 5.0. Regarding the ChatGPT
configuration, GPT-4 Turbo preview model (gpt-4-1106-preview)
was used through the OpenAI API. To mimic the two human raters
in the original TOEFL11 dataset, ChatGPT rated each essay twice
with different temperature values. It rated once with temperature
0 for more predictable and consistent responses, and again with
temperature 0.7 for more variability. We then took the average of
these two ratings to produce the final level for each essay. We used
a specific system prompt that instructed ChatGPT to act as a rater
for a high-stakes English language exam, providing the TOEFL iBT
Test Independent Writing Rubrics as a reference (See appendices A
and B for the full system prompts and user message).

4 Results
4.1 The rule-based approach
The rule-based approach served as a baseline and helped identify potentially important features. Table 1 shows that the bestperformed feature was the number of words (‘DESWC’) in an essay
with QWK=0.1650, suggesting that essay length had a slight correlation with essay quality. The second and third best-performed
features came from TAALES, implying the importance of lexical sophistication in essay assessment. The diversity of content
words (‘KF_Ncats_CW’) and the use of concrete language (‘Brysbaert_Concreteness_Combined_AW’) were predictive of quality.
The fourth feature, misspelling per 100 words (‘misspelling_per_100
_words’), showed that fewer spelling errors correlated with higher
essay levels. The fifth feature, academic lemma construction typetoken ratio (‘acad_lemma_construction_ttr’), suggested the positive
predictive power of variety of academic vocabulary on essay quality.
While these individual features showed some correlation with essay
levels, the QWK values are considered relatively low (below 0.2).

The ML approach

The limited performance of the rule-based approach calls for more
complex models in predicting essay quality. In the ML approach, we

Discussion

The findings of this study offer valuable insights into the effectiveness of rule-based, ML and LLM approaches in predicting essay
quality. The rule-based and statistical approach served as a baseline. While features such as essay length, lexical sophistication,
and spelling accuracy correlated with quality, their QWK values
remained low (below 0.2). This suggests that no single feature was
sufficient and that a more sophisticated approach is needed to capture the complexities of essay evaluation. For such an approach, one

This study also has implications for educational practice. First,
features such as essay and sentence length, word choice and spelling
that were found to be better predictors of scores (as measured using
the TOEFL dataset) could be prioritised in the writing classroom
to improve student performance. Second, due to their accessibility
and strengths in certain areas of AWE, LLMs could be employed
as a complementary tool in the writing process to offer support in
formative and low-stakes assessment. Although the scores given
by LLMs are difficult to explain due to their ‘black-box’ nature,
students could benefit from receiving qualitative feedback. Many
recent studies have already suggested the benefits of using LLMs
in the writing classroom (e.g. [18, 22]).

Limitations and future work

This study has several limitations. First, it focused exclusively on
TOEFL academic essays, which may not represent a full diversity
of writing styles and genres. Certain writing features could be
more significant in this specific context, potentially skewing results.
Another limitation is the use of basic linguistic features in ML
models, excluding advanced text representations such as TF-IDF or
complex embeddings. This restriction likely hindered the models’
ability to capture nuanced aspects of essay quality. In contrast,
GPT’s pre-training on extensive text data enables it to utilise rich,
contextual language representations, resulting in more effective
evaluations. Lastly, the selected ML models were trained on only
10% of the available essays, using an 80/20 train-test split. This
limited training data may have put the ML models at a disadvantage
compared to GPT, which benefits from extensive pre-training on
diverse texts.
For future work, integrating advanced deep learning architectures and exploring sophisticated text representation methods could
enhance ML models’ ability to capture relevant linguistic features
for essay evaluation. In addition, more detailed comparative studies
could offer deeper insights into specific conditions or essay types
where one method may outperform others. Future studies could
compare more LLMs and their scoring capabilities in different genres and different settings (e.g. with different prompts). Such studies
could pinpoint precise strengths and weaknesses and contribute to
more targeted improvements of AWE systems.

7

Conclusion

This study on AWE methods has yielded telling results, showing
clear differences in performance among the three approaches investigated: rule-based, ML, and LLM approaches. These results offer
insight into the most effective methods for reliable and accurate
essay scoring. While this study demonstrates the superiority of
GPT-based scoring in QWK and Pearson’s correlation coefficient,
it also points to the potential of advanced ML approaches in AWE.
As LLMs continue to develop, their potential is also expected to
grow. The implementations of AWE systems in the future will likely
involve an informed combination of different computational methods to balance performance with practical considerations. As AWE
methods continue to evolve, maintaining a focus on accuracy and
reliability, interpretability, and pedagogical value will be crucial in
developing systems that can enhance learning experiences.
