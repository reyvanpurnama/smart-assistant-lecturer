Advancements in generative AI, such as large language models (LLMs), may serve as a potential solution to the
burdensome task of essay grading often faced by language education teachers. Yet, the validity and reliability of
leveraging LLMs for automatic essay scoring (AES) in language education is not well understood. To address this,
we evaluated the cross-sectional and longitudinal validity and reliability of four prominent LLMs, Google’s PaLM
2, Anthropic’s Claude 2, and OpenAI’s GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, for the AES of English language learners’ writing. 119
essays taken from an English language placement test were assessed twice by each LLM, on two separate oc­
casions, as well as by a pair of human raters. GPT-4 performed the best, demonstrating excellent intrarater
reliability and good validity. All models, with the exception of GPT-3.5, improved over time in their intrarater
reliability. The interrater reliability of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, however, decreased slightly over time. These findings
indicate that some models perform better than others in AES and that all models are subject to fluctuations in
their performance. We discuss potential reasons for such variability, and offer suggestions for prospective ave­
nues of research.

1. Introduction
One of the earliest papers on automated essay scoring (AES) laments
the plight of English teachers who labor under the burden of “exorbi­
tant” grading responsibilities and how computerized essay scoring could
prove a brilliant solution (Page, 1966). 50 years later objections over
teacher workload persist, as do dreams of offloading that work to
automation (Godwin-Jones, 2022). Furthermore, shifting emphasis on
national standards, increased class sizes, and grade inflation have
contributed to a decline in student writing ability over the decades
(Carter & Harper, 2013; Singleton-Jackson et al., 2009). Teachers are
typically reluctant to assign more writing practice as doing so requires
the time to read and respond that teachers do not have (Page, 2002). A
reliable method of automated scoring of writing seems to be the obvious,
yet elusive solution for both reducing teacher workload and allowing for
more writing practice for students.
Testing services such as Educational Testing Service (ETS) and exams
like the Test of English as a Foreign Language (TOEFL) already employ
artificial intelligence (AI) integrated AES tools in their scoring proced­
ures, enabling them to process huge numbers of essays annually. Com­
mercial AES tools, such as Grammarly, have also adopted AI to provide

formative feedback on writing. However, all of these programs are
limited to specific features of writing (e.g., grammatical accuracy, word
choice, style) (Ramesh & Sanampudi, 2021), and are typically restricted
to academic genres of writing, such as the essay (Warschauer & Ware,
2006). These limitations, in addition to their cost, have stymied their
adoption in the classroom.
Recent advances in generative AI may offer a solution to the short­
comings of mainstream AES systems and finally address Page’s (1966)
complaint of excessive teacher workload, and other drawbacks, to the
manual scoring and feedback provision of student writing at the class­
room level. Generative AI systems, like OpenAI’s GPT-4, are
transformer-based deep neural network large language models (LLMs)
developed with large amounts of text from the Internet (OpenAI,
2023a). The domain-general nature of the training text for these systems
gives them much more versatility in the genres of text they can interact
with. Additionally, generative AI is not restricted to specific scoring
metrics and can instead analyze a text based on whatever parameters are
supplied by the user. Perhaps most importantly, many generative AI
tools are free and easy to use, making them accessible to both students
and teachers.

The interface of generative AI tools allows users to provide prompts
that explicitly outline the implementation parameters for a task. These
prompts can be optimized iteratively through prompt engineering (Zhou
et al., 2023). Therefore, it is theoretically possible to have ChatGPT, for
example, score an essay via the same features that mainstream AES

specific curriculum. However, due to the non-deterministic nature of
generative AI, the question of whether these systems can reliably adhere
to prompt specifications for AES needs to be addressed.
There is also the question of whether machines should be scoring
essays at all. Most arguments against AES are contingent on the purpose
for the writing. For example, some say that AES deprives writers of an
audience and infringes on the social nature of writing (Shermis et al.,
2013). Ericsson (2006) argues that writing conveys meaning more art­
fully than can be conceptualized by an AES algorithm. These arguments
have merit, and practitioners should consider carefully the ramifications
of submitting student work to AES systems. However, artful writing that
is done for the purpose of conveying meaning is one of many motives for
writing. Learners of additional languages, alternatively, perform writing
as a means of developing language proficiency and are therefore
assessed for their proficiency over the quality of their ideas (Weigle,
2013). This type of writing may be more conducive to AES, which is
proficient at identifying sentence-level errors in grammar and usage.
Exams for language education program placement or workplace certi­
fication can benefit from the use of AES to quickly and reliably score
essays along proficiency metrics.
Early research into using generative AI for AES and feedback pro­
vision have shown positive results. In a preprint by Dai et al. (2023),
researchers used ChatGPT to formulate feedback on 102 learner essays
and compared the feedback to the instructors. They found that ChatGPT
wrote more detailed and easier to read feedback than the teacher did,
but struggled to achieve a high level of agreement with the teacher’s
feedback on certain writing points.
In an experimental study, Mizumoto and Eguchi (2023) used the
IELTS task 2 writing band descriptors1 to score 12,100 TOEFL essays
using OpenAI’s text-davinci-003 model (a precursor of GPT-3.5) and
compared them to human ratings. They concluded that generative AI
tools like ChatGPT can accurately assess essays according to the rubric,
demonstrating the feasibility and efficacy of generative AI tools for AES.
In a related study, Escalante et al. (2023) employed GPT-4, OpenAI’s
newest and most powerful model at the time of writing, to provide
feedback on English language learner writing. They found that students
who received writing feedback generated by AI showed no differences in
learning outcomes and performance at the end of the semester from
those who received feedback from a human. Additionally, another group
of learners’ who received both AI and human feedback on their writing
were evenly split as to their preference, finding advantages with both.

2. Background & literature
AES involves analyzing written work and applying an evaluation
(typically a numerical score) through the use of technology. This has
been seen as a solution to human scoring of writing which is not only
subject to noise and bias but takes a lot of time to do faithfully (Ramesh
& Sanampudi, 2021). The key concerns regarding AES are whether
machine scores are valid and reliable, and whether machines ought to be
used for scoring human writing.
AES has historically been viewed as a replacement for the human
scoring of essays and this has led developers of AES systems to validate
AES performance in comparison to human scorers. However, there is not
a good understanding of how humans undertake the essay evaluation
process (Attali, 2013). For example, Eckes (2008) found that human
scorers are inconsistent with which writing features they give weight to
when presented with a list of features. Drawing further confusion as to
what governs human essay scoring, another study found rater scores to
be significantly different even after standardization training (Weigle,
1998). Despite the opaque nature of the human scoring process, AES
systems have been able to produce remarkably similar scores with those
of human raters (e.g., Page, 1966; Ramineni et al., 2012). However,
mainstream AES systems do not read essays, nor analyze them, as a
human would (Ericsson, 2006), which invites doubt as to their validity.
The algorithms and models driving mainstream AES tools have
become more sophisticated over time, allowing the use of these systems
to become more prevalent, particularly in high-stakes exams. Early AES
systems, such as Page’s (1966) Project Essay Grade™, used regression
models, and more recent systems such as ETS’s e-rater® and Pearson’s
Intelligent Essay Assessor™ have employed machine learning techniques
(Warschauer & Ware, 2006). These methods all require the preselection
of specific features of writing for analysis. For example, grammatical
accuracy, lexical variety, and semantic similarity are some common
features which are extracted from analyzed essays and compared to a
corpus of human-scored essays or defined along other algorithmic pa­
rameters (e.g., the ratio of unique word frequency to total words as a
measure of lexical variety) (Attali, 2013). In using these machine
learning techniques and focusing on certain writing features, machines
have been able to reliably produce essay scores that correlate highly
with those given by humans (Hussein et al., 2019). However, validity
along these terms has been challenged as superficial correlation (Per­
elman, 2014). If these criticisms are true, validity estimates that
compare human and machine scores are tenuous, as humans and ma­
chines are likely not measuring the same constructs.
Recent groundbreaking advances in natural language processing and
transformer-based AI have opened up new possibilities in AES. Ac­
cording to a recent systematic review, since the public release of
generative AI tools, such as ChatGPT by OpenAI in November of 2022,
many researchers have speculated that a key affordance of these tools in
educational contexts would be for evaluating and scoring student
writing (Bahroun et al., 2023). But how they might overcome the val­
idity and reliability shortcomings of predecessor AES systems is yet
unknown.
Unlike previous AES systems, generative AI is not specifically trained
to score essays and therefore does not rely on analyzing texts for pre­
defined features unless it is specifically prompted to do so. This might
make generative AI more similar to humans when scoring writing in that
the mechanisms through which it may arrive at an essay score are nondeterministic (Ouyang et al., 2023). Specifically, LLMs use an algorithm
of probability, which simply produces the next most statistically likely

Given the feasibility of using generative AI LLMs like GPT-4 for AES,
and their ability to provide actionable feedback, an important next step
is in measuring the reliability of LLMs when used for AES. Numerous
LLMs have been made available to the public but their comparative
capabilities in AES are unknown. Therefore, the purpose of this paper is
to explore the reliability of mainstream generative AI LLMs in AES.
Understanding how well LLMs perform in prompt-based AES compared
to human scorers will provide valuable insight on the efficacy of using
these tools for AES as well as broader understanding of the comparative
abilities of the prominent LLMs. A key complication in assessing the
reliability of LLMs is that models are periodically updated with new
training data, and this can affect their performance (Chen et al., 2023).
As such, the following research questions guided the study.
1. To what degree do LLMs exhibit intrarater reliability in automated
essay scoring?
2. How does the intrarater reliability of the LLMs change over time?
3. To what degree are LLMs’ scores valid, as exhibited by interrater
reliability with human scores?
4. How does the interrater reliability of the LLMs change over time?

3.3. Data analysis procedures

3. Methodology

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was selected to compare
inter and intra-rater reliabilities, as recommended for cases using
ordinal scales (de Raadt et al., 2021; Shrout & Fleiss, 1979). The ICC is
an index of rater error variance commonly used to establish the reli­
ability of measurement tools given a sample of subjects and two or more
raters. It has been shown to be an appropriate indicator of agreement
among raters even when the assumption of normality is not met by, for
example, returning more conservative estimates for skewed distribu­
tions (Mehta et al., 2018).
Pearson’s correlations were used for comparisons between machine
and human scores, as is typical in AES research (Attali, 2013).
For interrater reliabilities, the scores of two human raters for each
essay were averaged, as well were the two scores generated by each
LLM. Proportional bias analyses (Bland & Altman, 1999) were con­
ducted to further verify the reliability of measurements across instances.
A paired-samples t-test was done to compare mean LLM scores at the
T1 and T2 scoring instances. Lastly, descriptive statistics for each scoring
attempt by instrument are given in Table B.1 in Appendix B. As the LLMs
are being developed iteratively, we provide the versions of the LLMs as
well as the dates of analysis in Table C.1 in Appendix C. All calculations
were made using SPSS 28.

3.1. Experimental setup and data
The study used data from the admissions test of a small liberal arts
university in the Asia-Pacific area. The dataset was composed of essays
from a cohort of 119 ESL learners who took a university admissions and
placement test to assess language proficiency. Students completed the
test online using the Canvas learning management system. Students
wrote their answer to an essay prompt within a textbox with access to
the learning management system’s basic grammar and spellchecker,
which functions similar to the grammar and spellchecker in Microsoft
Word. Students’ essays had an average word count of 287. Standard
ethical procedures of the educational institution were adhered to in
order to protect the applicants’ anonymity.
3.2. Data collection procedures
The mandatory admissions test was created in-house and adminis­
tered online. As part of the writing portion of this admissions test, ap­
plicants were given 30 min to write an opinion essay on whether
physical exercise should be a required part of every school day. Each
essay was scored, individually by two English for Academic Purposes
teachers who used a simple holistic rubric (see Table A.1 in Appendix A).
This rubric was created by language educators of the host institution for
the purposes of placing incoming students into appropriate levels of
English language classes. In total 10 teachers participated in the rating
process. All of these teachers hold graduate degrees in Teaching English
to Speakers of Other Languages (TESOL), applied linguistics, and/or
education, with an average of 18.3 years of experience. The teachers first
calibrated on a subset of 10 essays. If any raters had Pearson correlation
coefficients below 0.75, they received more training and recalibrated.
After calibration, the Pearson’s correlation coefficient for the essay
scores by two raters was significant at 0.749 (p < 0.001), indicating a
strong correlation between scores and suggesting reasonable inter-rater
reliability.
In addition, four LLMs were used to score the essays. These were
Google’s PaLM 2 (via chatbot Bard), Anthropic’s Claude 2, and OpenAI’s
GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (via chatbot ChatGPT). These LLMs are all
transformer-based, autoregressive language models that have been
trained on large internet corpora. These models were chosen because at
the time of data collection they were among the best performing and

Ethical concerns also merit further discussion. Mainstream AES
systems have been found to score essays differently along demographic
lines (Bridgeman et al., 2012; Ramineni & Williamson, 2018). A valid
concern is the potential for LLMs to also perpetuate biases stemming
from the material they are trained on. Such AI bias has been found in
employment (Bogen, 2019), healthcare (Obenmeyer et al., 2019),
criminal justice (Idder & Coulaux, 2021), and education (Baker & Hawn,
2021). Research exploring potential bias of LLMs when tasked with
evaluating student writing is needed. It may be possible, for example,
that because LLMs are trained on large datasets that may overrepresent
certain dialects or varieties of English, they might favor or be more ac­
curate with writing that aligns with these forms. This would potentially
put learners who use different structures or vocabulary, reflective of
their native language influences, at a disadvantage. Another ethical
concern is student privacy (Pack & Maloney, 2023b, 2024). Usage of
generative AI for language assessment needs to adhere to institutional
and governmental regulations and policies that protect student and
participant privacy by, for example, stripping any identifying informa­
tion provided to LLMs in student writing.
Also worth noting is that some of the arguments against AES persist
with the usage of LLMs. For example, some skepticism directed at
mainstream AES systems is that they are proprietary, and research into
them is restricted. Similarly, the developers of the chatbots and LLMs
used in this study, namely OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic, carefully
guard the algorithms behind these tools. It is also known that the de­
velopers of generative AI do not have a full understanding of the internal
workings of LLMs. The high dimensionality of deep neural networks
makes it difficult for humans to visualize how generative AI makes de­
cisions or handles new data; a phenomenon known as the black box
(Bathaee, 2018). Because of this, a degree of trust is necessary when
accepting any AI output.
This study contributes to the research on AES by making the first
exploration of the validity and reliability of LLMs for the automated
scoring of language learner writing. These new technologies are easy
and tempting to use in educational contexts and practitioners should be
informed of their limitations. Our findings indicate that certain LLMs
might presently be appropriate for limited AES. Their use as a second
rater alongside human raters or to resolve a disagreement between two
human raters might be acceptable, as might their use in low-stakes or
formative writing. If the limitations and ethical considerations presented
in this study can be addressed then it is undoubtable that LLMs will see
more widespread use in AES.
One limitation of our study is its use of a holistic rubric with a scoring
range of 1–6. Small ordinal scoring systems have been found to, at times,
exhibit correlations that are more likely to occur by chance (Gardner
et al., 2021). With the recent functionality of GPT-4 turbo, future studies
could leverage rubrics with more complex scoring systems.

One reason for why GPT-4 demonstrated higher interrater reliability

confined use of the scoring rubric.
While GPT-4 demonstrated high intra and interrater reliability in this
study, there are several reasons to be hesitant in applying it, or other
LLMs, for AES at this point. To begin with, several of the LLMs exhibited
notable quirks when assessing student writing. For instance, GPT-4,
GPT-3.5 and PaLM 2, on a few occasions assigned scores on a contin­
uous scale, despite the rubric being ordinal. For another example, there
were a few instances where students finished the writing portion of the
admissions test with an incomplete sentence lacking a full stop, GPT-4
would respond by finishing the students’ paragraph for them and then
assign a score, sometimes including its own writing in its evaluation of
the students’ writing, thereby awarding the writing with a higher score.
This was remedied by adding a full stop to the students’ writing, which
we did before conducting our analyses. Furthermore, as evidenced by
their scoring rationale (see Appendix D), the LLMs at times hallucinated
features of student writing. For instance, PaLM 2 erroneously claimed
that the student “developed 2–3 paragraphs” when the student’s writing
had no visible paragraphing. It is possible that these quirks can be
remedied using few-shot prompting or by adjusting the temperature
parameter, and future research should explore these possibilities.
Another reason to be cautious in using LLMs for AES is their nondeterministic nature; the same input given by a user will not generate
the same output, a fact that prompted the current study. Several factors
lead to this feature of generative AI, such as randomness in the sampling
methods and temperature settings (Ouyang et al., 2023). This, plus the
frequency at which models are updated, makes it impossible to make an
ultimate reliability judgment. Due to this, it is our recommendation that
practitioners using LLMs for AES conduct their own reliability analysis
specific to their educational context.
The success of using an LLM for assessing writing is also impacted by
factors such as the nature of the writing assignment, the rubric utilized
for scoring, and the token count required to engineer a successful
prompt. As such, the generalizability of GPT-4’s success in this study is
untenable until further evidence is acquired. For example, we have
found GPT-4 to be more successful with simple holistic rubrics, such as
the one used in this study that requires only one score as output, than

6. Conclusion
The advancement and refinement of LLMs is proceeding at an un­
precedented rate. As the technology evolves, we anticipate LLMs will
play an increasingly integral role in language assessment, especially if
LLMs are fine-tuned for the specific purpose of assessing language. The
5

A. Pack et al.

Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence 6 (2024) 100234

next generation of LLMs, such as OpenAI’s GPT-5 may prove to be even
more capable. In the near future we expect these generative AI tools to
be able to reliably, accurately, and rapidly assess hundreds or thousands
of writing samples in a short period. Furthermore, we anticipate the
trajectory of technological development of LLMs to outpace the rate of
academic research and publication, thereby posing both challenges and
opportunities for the pedagogical community. While optimistic about
this technology, we urge caution in using current LLMs for assessing
language. Prior to integrating LLMs into assessment processes, we
strongly urge language educators and program administrators to
conduct rigorous validity and reliability analyses to ascertain the
alignment of the LLM’s evaluations with those of human raters and with
themselves.
The importance of cross-disciplinary collaboration is increasingly
apparent at this intersection of technological advancement and peda­
gogical practice in the field of language assessment. In particular we see
several major research gaps that warrant investigation. First, we need to
better understand the capabilities of current LLMs. For example, while
GPT-4 was found to be the most valid and reliable model in the current
study, further examination of the GPT-4’s performance in other contexts
that utilize various writing assessments and rubrics would provide in­
sights into the versatility of the LLM and its validity and reliability across
contexts. Additionally, the capacity for LLMs to consistently and accu­
rately evaluate multiple students’ writing concurrently, by means of
uploaded.csv files, ought to be explored. Third, cross-disciplinary
collaborative efforts between computational linguists, machine
learning experts, and language assessment experts, to fine tune LLMs for
the purpose of assessing language would be of particular value as this
would likely improve the LLMs’ performance in this regard. Further­
more, as the utilization of LLMs for language assessment becomes more
widespread, it is of growing importance that the ethical concerns
highlighted above are addressed. In particular, it would be advanta­
geous to know if and to what degree LLMs may exhibit bias in their

assessment of learners’ language. To conclude, it is probable that
generative AI tools like LLMs will significantly transform language
assessment practices; it is therefore crucial to proactively engage with
the potential challenges and opportunities that they present.

accurately. The student develops 2–3 paragraphs fully, but the supporting detail is sometimes personal and not always concrete. The student uses

Specific feedback
The student should use more cohesive devices to connect their ideas. For example, they could use transition words like “however,” “therefore,” and
“in addition."

achieve."
