Abstract
The rapid diffusion of Large Language Model (LLM) technology is reshaping
educational software, yet most Automated Grading Platforms (AGPs) still restrict
feedback to binary verdicts or static hints. This lack of personalised, context-aware
guidance curtails their pedagogical impact, especially in introductory programming
courses where formative support is critical. Many students resort to uncontrolled
use of external AI assistants, which undermines the feedback loop and threatens
academic integrity.
This thesis investigates how AI-generated feedback can be integrated into
existing AGPs without entangling platform code with fast-evolving AI services. We
propose a provider-agnostic middleware that (i) decouples platform-specific details
through modular adapters, (ii) enables dynamic model selection, and (iii) supplies
a prompt-template system that could embed unit-test outcomes, code context,
and instructor goals into reproducible LLM prompts. A reference implementation
(built with Flask, MongoDB, and Docker) exposes a REST API and a lightweight
Python client, enabling educators to adopt the solution with minimal effort and to
experiment with alternative models or prompt strategies.
Effectiveness was evaluated in two validation phases within a first-year
computer-science course on INGInious. Across the two phases, AI feedback increased
the immediate success rate on the next attempt by +5.02 and +15.10 percentage
points, almost all students (94.4% in Survey 1 and 100% in Survey 2) agreed
that the tool can be an asset for improving programming learning, and over 75%
indicated that such a tool could reduce their use of external AI assistance.
These findings show that the proposed middleware transforms static AGP
verdicts into targeted formative feedback that improves learning outcomes, heightens
student satisfaction, and curbs unregulated AI usage, without sacrificing platform
maintainability. The main contributions are: (1) a reusable, provider-agnostic
middleware architecture; (2) an open-source implementation; and (3) a mixedmethod validation demonstrating statistically significant quantitative gains and
rich qualitative insights.

Chapter 1
Introduction
AGPs such as INGInious have become indispensable tools in large-scale educational
settings, enabling instructors to evaluate student submissions automatically and
at scale[1, 2, 3]. At the same time, recent advances in artificial intelligence,
most notably the emergence of LLMs, have made powerful, human-like languagegeneration capabilities widely accessible[2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. These developments open
the door to enriching automated grading with nuanced, context-aware feedback
that goes well beyond simple correctness checks[4, 5].
Despite this promise, existing AGPs typically struggle to offer the kind of
detailed, adaptive feedback that human graders provide; for example, comments
tailored to a student’s specific misconceptions or hints that guide problem-solving
without revealing complete solutions[3, 5]. This limitation not only reduces the
pedagogical value of automated grading, but also tempts students to bypass the
system by seeking uncontrolled, external AI assistance[10, 6, 7, 11].
Motivated by the need to enhance learning through personalized feedback
while maintaining academic integrity[12, 10, 11, 9], this thesis proposes a flexible,
middleware-based architecture that integrates AI feedback directly into existing
AGPs. By placing an intermediary layer between the grading engine and one
or more LLM providers, the middleware can be developed independently of the
AGP itself. This architecture enables educators to add AI-powered feedback to
programming exercises and to customize prompt templates in accordance with
their instructional guidelines, thereby curbing the uncontrolled use of off-the-shelf
AI tools and ensuring that comments remain pedagogically aligned with course
objectives.
The primary objectives of this work include designing and implementing an AIpowered feedback system capable of generating detailed, context-aware comments
on coding exercises; integrating this system into the INGInious autograder via a
modular Flask-based middleware layer; and empirically validating the effectiveness
of the resulting architecture through both qualitative and quantitative studies.

feedback records; a lightweight Python client package for seamless integration with
AGPs; and Docker-based containerization to facilitate reproducible deployment.
The middleware’s prompt system leverages parameterized templates and dynamic
model selection, enabling controlled experimentation with multiple LLMs under

This thesis makes the following contributions: identification of the pedagogical
gap between simple correctness checking and context-aware feedback in existing
automated grading platforms; a novel middleware framework that cleanly decouples
grading logic from AI feedback generation, supporting multiple platforms and AI
providers; a full-stack implementation comprising a Flask API, MongoDB schema,
Python client library, prompt management module, and Docker orchestration for
seamless deployment; design of a prompt template for AI-generated educational
feedback following prompt-engineering best practices; and a mixed-methods empirical evaluation assessing feedback quality and system performance, accompanied
by a critical discussion of limitations and threats to validity.
The remainder of this document is structured as follows. Chapter 2 reviews

Chapter 5 describes the concrete software implementation, covering the technology
stack, database schema, API endpoints, and client library. Chapter 6 presents the
application of our system to a variety of coding exercises and the prompt engineering
process. Chapter 7 reports on the validation study, with quantitative results,
qualitative analyses, and a discussion of threats to validity. Finally, Chapter 8
concludes by reflecting on the extent to which the objectives were met and Chapter 9
outlines avenues for future work.

Background Material
In this chapter, we introduce the essential concepts and technical vocabulary
that underpin this master thesis. We start by examining the state of the art
in programming-centric Automated Grading Platform (AGP), using INGInious
as our primary example to illustrate common architectures and workflows. We

2.1

Automated Grading Platforms (AGPs)

Automated Grading Platforms are software systems designed to evaluate student
work, particularly programming assignments, reducing instructor workload and
providing rapid, objective feedback. In this thesis we focus on programming-centric
AGPs, with INGInious serving as our primary case study.

General Landscape of Autograding
Contemporary programming AGPs typically follow a four-step workflow. First,
students submit their code via a web interface. Second, each submission is sandboxed in an isolated environment such as a Docker container to ensure security.
Third, dynamic testing methods (for example, unit tests or simulation) and static
analysis compare program behavior and code metrics against expected results. Finally, grades and diagnostic information, including error traces and style hints, are
aggregated and returned to students, enabling iterative resubmission and solution
refinement.
3

Figure 2.1: Example architecture of a programming AGP.
Programming AGPs offer several advantages. Their scalability allows them
to handle large volumes of submissions with minimal additional instructor effort,
while instant feedback ensures that students receive assessments and diagnostics
immediately, supporting a rapid learning cycle. Automated criteria maintain
consistency and fairness across all submissions.

The INGInious Platform
INGInious is an open-source programming AGP developed at the Université
catholique de Louvain (UCL). It comprises a Python-based backend, lightweight
agents that launch Docker containers for each submission, and a web frontend
for both students and instructors. Instructors supply an evaluation script which
compiles, executes and tests each submission within its container and generates
both grades and detailed feedback traces. INGInious was chosen as the direct
target of this thesis due to my affiliation with UCL and ongoing collaboration with
its developers.

2.2

Large Language Models (LLMs)

LLMs are neural networks, typically based on the transformer architecture, trained
on massive text corpora to predict the next token in a sequence. By learning
statistical patterns of language at scale, these models acquire capabilities ranging
from syntax and semantics understanding to commonsense reasoning and basic
problem-solving. Their ability to generate coherent, contextually appropriate text
has underpinned the recent surge in AI applications, from conversational agents to
automated code review.

Transformers and the AI Revolution
Transformers, introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017)[13], rely on self-attention
mechanisms to model dependencies across long text spans. Unlike earlier recurrent
or convolutional models, transformers can be scaled to billions of parameters and
trained in parallel, yielding substantial gains in fluency and reasoning. As compute
and data availability grew, successive generations of transformer-based LLMs,
notably the GPT family, demonstrated that "pre-training" on broad text followed
by "fine-tuning" or "instruction tuning" could produce models capable of tackling
diverse tasks with minimal additional training.

Pre-training on Code Corpora
Modern LLMs are routinely pre-trained on mixed datasets that include substantial
volumes of source code (e.g., GitHub repositories, programming Q&A forums).
Empirical studies show that incorporating code during pre-training confers dramatic
improvements: for instance, OpenAI’s Codex (a GPT variant fine-tuned on GitHub)
solved 28.8% of HumanEval Python problems versus 0% for the text-only GPT3 base model [14]. A systematic analysis reports up to a 12× boost in coding
performance and concurrent gains in natural language reasoning when code is
included in the training mix [15]. Learning from code exposes the model to precise
syntactic rules, programming idioms, and logical reasoning patterns that transfer
to both code and non-code tasks.

Reasoning-Enhanced Variants
While base LLMs excel at pattern completion, they may struggle with multi-step
logic. To address this, "reasoning" variants have been developed with specialized
training objectives or architectures that encourage explicit chain-of-thought generation and tool use. These models, sometimes referred to as "O-series" (OpenAI) or

5

R1 (DeepSeek), are optimized to decompose complex problems into intermediate
steps, improving performance on maths, logic puzzles, and code generation.

Major Providers in the LLM Ecosystem
The LLM landscape is diverse, with several key players offering models that differ
in scale, cost, context capacity, and reasoning capability. Here we summarize the
most notable providers:
OpenAI pioneered the commercial LLM market with the GPT series. GPT-4
and its successors set benchmarks in conversational quality and coding prowess,
while the O-series models (O1, O3, O4-mini) introduced explicit reasoning and
tool-use training. Despite their high performance, these models carry premium
pricing, motivating the emergence of leaner alternatives.
Anthropic’s Claude models (e.g., Claude 3.5 and 3.7) introduced support for
large context windows up to 200 k tokens at launch and were among the first to
support agentic tool calling. Developers appreciate these models for their robust
coding performance. [16]
Google’s Gemini line arrived later but quickly closed the gap. Gemini 2.0
Flash offers high throughput at low cost, while Gemini 2.5 Pro matches top-tier
reasoning performance with multi-million-token contexts[17].
DeepSeek disrupted expectations by open-sourcing its R1 reasoning models,
which achieve reasoning and coding performance comparable to much larger proprietary systems, yet at a fraction of the resource cost[18].
Mistral AI a European startup, delivers compact open-source models like Mistral
Medium 3, which balance state-of-the-art reasoning and coding performance with
up to 8× lower cost and 128 k-token context windows.
Together, these providers form a diverse ecosystem of LLMs differing in scale,
cost, context capacity, and reasoning capability.

2.3

Prompt Engineering

Prompt engineering is the art and science of designing the natural-language instructions (prompts) sent to a LLM so as to elicit clear, accurate, and useful
responses[19][20]. By carefully structuring the prompt, including a clear task
6

description, relevant context, and a specified output format, practitioners can steer
the model toward better performance without additional training.

Core Prompting Techniques
Three widely used strategies illustrate the range of prompting approaches:
Zero-Shot Prompting In zero-shot prompting, the model is given only a task
description and the input, with no examples. For instance:
“Review this Python function and explain any logical errors in two
sentences:
def sum_list(xs): return xs[0] + xs[-1]”
This approach is simple but may struggle with more complex or nuanced tasks[19].
Few-Shot Prompting Few-shot prompting provides a small number of exemplars
before the target input. Example:
“Example 1:
Input: ...
Feedback: Good use of list indexing, but it fails on empty lists.
Example 2:
Input: ...
Feedback: Consider using .lower() for case-insensitive comparison.
Now review this submission: ...”
By showing correct input–output pairs, few-shot can greatly improve relevance and
accuracy at the cost of longer prompts and higher token usage[19].
Chain-of-Thought Prompting Chain-of-thought prompting asks the model to
“think aloud,” generating intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final
answer. For example:
“Q: The function foo crashes on empty input lists. Why?
A: Let me think step by step: first, the code attempts to access xs[0],
which...
...
Therefore, the error occurs because ...”
This technique has been shown to improve performance on multi-step reasoning
tasks, such as debugging algorithms or explaining code behavior[21].
7

Relation to This Thesis While this thesis applies only zero-shot prompting to
generate feedback on student submissions, the principles of few-shot and chain-ofthought prompting are relevant for future extensions. Few-shot prompting could
enhance feedback specificity by providing examples of desired commentary styles or
common errors, while chain-of-thought prompting could help the model articulate
its reasoning process, leading to more insightful explanations. Exploration of these
richer techniques is deferred to future work (see Chapter 9).

Best Practices
Several general principles help ensure that prompts are effective and efficient[22,
23, 24, 25, 26, 27].
• Instruction First: Begin with a concise directive that specifies the task
before any context or examples, to focus model attention on the primary
objective.
• Define Output Structure: Explicitly indicate the desired format, such
as JSON, bullet points, or numbered lists, and, if useful, include a minimal
example to exemplify the structure.
• Be Explicit and Concise: Use precise language to convey style constraints
(e.g., tone, length) and content focus (e.g., grammar correction vs. semantic
feedback), avoiding ambiguity.
• Optimize Model Parameters: Adjust parameters such as model variant,
temperature (e.g., zero for deterministic outputs), max tokens, top_p, and
stop sequences to align the generation behavior with your use case.
• Use Markdown for Clarity: Leverage Markdown formatting (headings,
lists, code blocks) to structure prompts and expected outputs, improving
readability and parsing.
• Provide Relevant Context: Supply necessary background information or
definitions upfront to ground the model and reduce misunderstanding, while
keeping context concise.
• Iterate and Refine: Test prompts against representative inputs, analyze
outputs, and iteratively adjust wording and parameters to improve consistency
and quality.

8

Application in This Thesis In Chapters 5 and 6, all zero-shot prompts are
crafted according to the above best practices, ensuring clear, concise, and deterministic feedback generation. This approach lays a foundation that can seamlessly
accommodate few-shot or chain-of-thought strategies in future extensions.
These prompting concepts form the foundation for crafting templates that can
be adapted to a wide range of tasks, including code review, explanatory feedback,
or creative text generation, without modifying the underlying model parameters.

2.4

Middleware & the Mediator Design Pattern

Middleware is a software layer that sits between two or more applications or services,
handling communication, translation and coordination tasks without modifying the
core logic of any endpoint. By abstracting common functionality into a separate
component, middleware promotes cleaner designs and simplifies both development
and maintenance.
In software engineering, this centralized orchestration role aligns directly with
the Mediator Design Pattern [28], which defines an object that encapsulates
how a set of objects interact. The Mediator promotes loose coupling by preventing
components from referring to each other explicitly, and lets their interactions vary
independently. In this thesis, we will leverage the Mediator pattern to centralize
communication between the AGP and AI services (see Chapter 4).
A primary benefit of decoupling via middleware is that changes on one side
need not ripple through all components. For example, if a new AI service requires
a different authentication scheme or payload format, only the middleware need be
updated; the grading platform and downstream business logic remain untouched.
This separation of concerns also enables independent testing and versioning of each
layer.
Middleware often centralizes cross-cutting services such as logging, metrics
collection, authentication, rate limiting and retry logic. Consolidating these aspects
in one place ensures consistent behavior across all integrations and provides a single
point for observability and error handling.

Chapter 3
Related Work
In this chapter, we survey prior research on Automated Grading Platforms (AGPs),
the application of LLMs to generate artifacts and feedback, learner and AI interaction patterns, and integrated platforms that bring these components together.
We begin by reviewing some of the foundational work in automated grading, then
explore how LLMs have been used to generate programming exercises and test
suites. Next, we discuss formative feedback enabled by LLMs, including strategies
to avoid direct solutions and empirical studies of student interactions with AI
support. We then examine end-to-end AI-enhanced platforms that ground feedback in course content and enforce integrity. Finally, we touch upon the practical
challenges observed in deploying LLM-based evaluators. This overview highlights
key gaps and sets the stage for our proposed middleware architecture.

3.1

Foundations of Automated Grading

The concept of automated grading dates back to the mid-1960s, when systems used
batch compilers and instructor-written test suites to deliver pass/fail results at
scale [29].
Static analysis tools later added style and complexity checks, but these methods
still provide only binary or generic feedback, limiting their pedagogical value.
Subsequent developments in static analysis leveraged abstract syntax tree pattern
matching and constraint-based techniques to localize syntactic and semantic errors,
offering more informative diagnostics but still falling short of personalized formative
guidance [1].

11

Relevance to our work
While these early systems established the feasibility and scalability of automated
grading, their lack of adaptive, explanatory feedback motivates our middleware’s
integration of AI-generated comments. Unlike traditional AST-based diagnostics
that only pinpoint errors, our approach aims to deliver context-aware explanations
and scaffolded hints tailored to individual student submissions, as detailed in
Chapter 4.

3.2

LLM-Driven Artifact Generation

LLMs have been applied to generate programming exercises and test suites. GPT-4
can produce C test suites that match instructor verdicts with over 99% precision
and expose specification ambiguities [2]. Codex1 has been used to author hundreds
of problems (including descriptions, solutions, and unit tests) with a majority
deemed sensible and executable [3]. Additionally, recent work has combined LLMgenerated test suites with dynamic analysis to iteratively refine edge-case coverage,
demonstrating the potential for automated tools to improve test completeness and
reveal hidden specification gaps.
Relevance to our work These studies demonstrate the potential of using LLMs
in education to reduce instructor workload. Our approach similarly aims to alleviate
instructor burden and enhance the student experience by generating personalized
feedback.

3.3

Formative Feedback via LLMs

Recent work directly compares LLM-generated feedback to human feedback, examines student perceptions, and explores novel ways to deliver feedback that fosters
deeper learning.

Performance Comparison
In a study on recursion quizzes, GPT-4 feedback led to significantly higher mean
resubmission scores (M = 4.33 for GPT-4 vs. M = 4.02 for instructor), indicating
greater student code improvement as scores (with 5 being correct) reflected enhanced
accuracy. GPT-4 also demonstrated superior error capture in initial student
submissions (90.8% vs. 75.6%)[4]. These findings indicate that with well-engineered

prompts, LLM-based feedback can at least match, and in some metrics exceed,
human feedback in guiding student code revisions.

Perceptions and Prompting
In an introductory Java course, 72.5% of students preferred feedback prompts that
included their own code, associating this with greater specificity and clarity. Over
95% agreed the feedback was unbiased and presented in manageable units, though
29% suggested deeper explanations or illustrative examples for clarity enhancements
[5]. These insights underscore the role of prompt engineering: few-shot exemplars,
student-code contexts, and tone parameters can be leveraged to optimize relevance
and student satisfaction.

Strategies for Avoiding Direct Solutions and Fostering Deeper
Learning
A key concern with LLM-generated feedback is the potential for students to receive
direct solutions, hindering conceptual engagement. Several systems have been
developed to address this. CodeAid, an LLM-powered programming assistant, was
designed to deliver helpful and technically correct responses without revealing code
solutions, instead offering conceptual explanations, pseudo-code, and annotations
on student’s incorrect code [11]. Similarly, Sakshm AI employs a chatbot, Disha,
that uses a Socratic questioning framework to provide context-aware hints and
adaptive guidance while maintaining conversational memory, explicitly avoiding
direct code generation [9]. These approaches often utilize guardrails and scaffolding
techniques, such as interactive pseudo-code and code annotations, to guide students
from understanding concepts to independently writing and debugging their code.
Educators have generally responded positively to such pedagogical approaches,
valuing the emphasis on critical thinking.

Meta-Analyses of ChatGPT in Education
Wang and Fan conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis of 51 quasi-experimental
studies published between November 2022 and February 2025. They report that
ChatGPT use yields a large positive effect on students’ learning performance, and
moderate positive effects on learning perception and higher-order thinking [8].
For learning performance, significant moderators included course type
(strongest in skills/competencies development), learning model (largest effect in
problem-based learning), and duration (optimal at 4–8 weeks). Grade level, ChatGPT’s role, or application area were not significant moderators for this outcome.

13

The impact on learning perception was significantly moderated solely by
intervention duration, with positive effects strengthening with prolonged use,
particularly over 8 weeks.
For higher-order thinking, significant differences emerged based on the type
of course (most impactful in STEM-related courses) and the role of ChatGPT
(most effective when acting as an intelligent tutor). Learning model, duration,
or application area did not significantly moderate this effect. These findings
underscore that while generally beneficial, ChatGPT’s impact is maximized in
specific educational contexts and applications.

Practical Challenges and Student Perceptions of LLM Evaluators
While LLMs show promise, their practical application in real-world classrooms for
assignment evaluation has revealed challenges. Chiang et al. report on a largescale deployment where GPT-4 was used as an "LLM TA" (LLM-based evaluation
teaching assistant) to evaluate assignments, with students having free access to
this evaluator and submitting the LLM’s evaluation as their grade. While generally
acceptable to students, issues arose: 51% of students found the LLM TA failed
to correctly follow output format instructions, and 22% observed that it did not
always adhere to evaluation criteria. A significant concern was prompt hacking,
with 47% of students attempting to manipulate the LLM TA for higher scores; these
attempts were often successful, though detectable post-hoc [12]. This highlights
the vulnerability of LLM evaluators and the need for robust defense mechanisms
and clear institutional policies.

Relevance to our work
These studies validate the potential of LLMs to enhance student learning through
personalized feedback and illustrate various strategies for achieving this. Our
middleware leverages these insights by embedding LLMs into grading workflows,
allowing for adaptive, context-aware responses that align with pedagogical goals.
The work on CodeAid and Sakshm AI reinforces our focus on avoiding overly direct
solutions and fostering student thinking. The challenges highlighted by Chiang
et al. underscore the importance of our proposed system’s features for prompt
management, security, and rate limiting to maintain academic integrity and ensure
reproducible, reliable feedback.

3.4

Learner–AI Interaction Patterns

Empirical logs reveal how students interact with and use AI support, highlighting
both opportunities and risks.

Overreliance and Shallow Engagement
Amoozadeh et al. observed that roughly one-third of AI-assisted sessions involved
no independent student effort—learners often pasted entire problem statements
into the model and accepted its output without verification, leading to shallow
engagement and diminished problem-solving practice [6]. This overreliance risks
reinforcing misconceptions rather than promoting genuine understanding. Studies
like CodeAid also found that while many students displayed self-regulation, some
expressed concerns about over-relying on the tool [11].

Self-Regulation Versus Over-Trust
Kazemitabaar et al. analyzed novice users of a Codex-based generator in a selfpaced Python course and found a tension between productive tinkering and blind
acceptance. While some students decomposed problems, tested incrementally, and
refined AI suggestions, many others skipped verification steps entirely, inadvertently
amplifying errors in their code [7]. The Socratic approach of systems like Sakshm
AI aims to foster such self-regulatory behaviors by guiding students with hints
rather than answers [9].

Scaffolding Design Implications
Together, these findings suggest that effective tool design must include explicit
scaffolds for help-seeking: prompting learners to decompose tasks, encouraging
iterative testing of AI output, and incorporating verification checks into the workflow.
Such scaffolding can mitigate overreliance, support deeper engagement, and promote
transfer of problem-solving skills beyond AI-generated hints [6, 7]. The design of
CodeAid, with its emphasis on pseudo-code and interactive explanations, reflects
these principles [11].

Prompt Engineering Implications
This insight guided our prompt engineering: we designed feedback prompts so they
never supply complete solutions that students could copy–paste. Instead, prompts
offer directional hints that encourage learners to tackle problems independently.
This approach aims to foster deeper engagement and self-regulation, aligning with
15

the findings of Amoozadeh et al., Kazemitabaar et al., and the design philosophy
of systems like CodeAid [11] and Sakshm AI [9].

3.5

Integrated AI-Enhanced Platforms

End-to-end platforms now leverage AI to personalize learning, automate grading,
and deliver instant hints. For example, CS50.ai combines code explanation extensions, style diffing, and a GPT-4 chatbot grounded in lecture content, using
retrieval augmentation and interaction guardrails to maintain accuracy and integrity [10]. An LLM-powered "helper bot" integrated into both an IDE plugin
and command-line autograder has similarly shown promise in offering concise,
pedagogically aligned hints without revealing complete solutions [30]. Moreover,
these platforms often incorporate prompt-injection detectors and dynamic retrieval
pipelines to reduce hallucinations and uphold academic integrity.
Another approach involves AI augmenting the capabilities of instructional
staff. BoilerTAI, for instance, integrates Generative Artificial Intelligence with
educational forums, allowing TAs to review, refine, and approve AI-generated
draft responses to student questions, thereby reducing teaching assistant workload
while maintaining quality control through a human-in-the-loop system [31]. While
focused on a different interaction point (forum vs. AGP), this highlights the theme
of integrating AI into existing educational workflows with human oversight.

Relevance to our work
These platforms demonstrate the potential of integrating AI feedback with course
content and grading workflows. However, many platforms remain tied to specific
ecosystems or, like BoilerTAI, serve different purposes such as TA support rather
than direct AGP feedback. Our middleware architecture aims to provide a more
universal, provider-agnostic solution to decouple core autograding logic from AI
feedback engines, enabling scalable and pedagogically adaptive integrations across
various AGPs, as detailed in Chapter 4.

3.6

Conclusion

The preceding survey has traced the evolution of automated grading from early
batch-compiler and static-analysis systems to contemporary LLM-driven approaches
for exercise generation, test-suite synthesis, and context-aware formative feedback.
While traditional tools scale well for binary or generic judgment, they lack the
nuanced, personalized guidance that recent language models can deliver. Empirical

16

comparisons show that LLM-based comments can match or exceed human feedback
in error detection and clarity. Systems like CodeAid and Sakshm AI demonstrate
strategies for providing rich feedback without direct solutions, fostering deeper
learning and self-regulation. Learner–AI interaction logs underscore risks of overreliance and shallow engagement when scaffolding is absent, but also highlight
the potential for AI to support productive tinkering and critical thinking when
designed appropriately.
End-to-end AI-enhanced platforms demonstrate the potential of integrating
profiling, grading, and hinting, but often remain tied to specific ecosystems or
focus on different interaction points like Forum support. The practical challenges
observed in deploying LLM-based evaluators, such as prompt hacking and adherence
to evaluation criteria, highlight the need for robust defense mechanisms and clear
institutional policies.
To address these gaps and opportunities, Chapter 4 presents a middleware architecture that decouples core autograding logic from interchangeable AI feedback
engines, enabling more scalable and pedagogically adaptive integrations across
diverse platforms. This design, informed by the successes and challenges of prior
work, aims to deliver context-aware, instructionally sound feedback while maintaining academic integrity and platform independence. It is complemented by a
user-centered evaluation to assess its effectiveness and to better understand which
risks and challenges may still persist.

Chapter 4
Proposed Architecture
This chapter presents our conceptual design and high-level architecture for integrating AI-generated feedback into Automated Grading Platforms (AGPs). It outlines
the main components, their interactions, and their core responsibilities, setting the
stage for a more detailed implementation discussion in the following chapter.
Our primary goal is to enhance traditional AGPs, exemplified by INGInious
in this thesis, with scalable and pedagogically valuable feedback generated by
LLMs. The core idea is to introduce a dedicated middleware layer that acts as an
intermediary, connecting, on the one hand, the automated grading environment
and, on the other hand, various AI models.
This approach, which centralizes communication control within the middleware,
is a practical application of the Mediator design pattern (see Section 2.4). It aims
for modularity and flexibility, allowing integration with different AGPs and different
AI providers while abstracting away the complexities of direct AI interaction.

4.1

System Architecture

The proposed system architecture comprises three main conceptual components:
the AGP (e.g., INGInious), the central middleware server, and one or more external
AI models. Figure 4.1 provides a high-level overview of these components and their
interactions.
The basic workflow for generating feedback on student’s code submissions
proceeds as follows:
1. Submission: A student submits their solution (e.g., source code) to an
exercise via the AGP.
2. Grading: The platform performs its standard evaluation, typically running
automated unit tests.
18

Figure 4.1: High-level system architecture for AI-based feedback integration.
3. Data Forwarding: After grading (or potentially in parallel, depending
on the configuration), the platform sends relevant information, such as the
student’s code, exercise description, test results, and potentially instructor
guidelines, to the middleware.
4. Middleware Processing: The middleware receives and stores the submission data. It then selects an appropriate AI model and constructs a carefully
designed prompt based on the received data and pre-configured instructions
(see Section 5.6 for details on the prompt mechanism).
5. AI Feedback Generation: The middleware sends the generated prompt to
the chosen AI model. The AI model processes the request and returns the
generated feedback.
6. Feedback Handling: The middleware receives the feedback from the AI
model, stores it for logging and potential analysis, and performs any necessary
post-processing (e.g., formatting).
7. Feedback Delivery: The middleware sends the processed feedback back to
the AGP.
8. Display to Student: Finally, the AGP displays the AI-generated feedback
to the student, typically alongside the traditional results from the automated
unit tests provided by the AGP.

19

This architecture decouples the AGP from the details of AI model interactions, thereby enhancing both maintainability and extensibility. This design choice
was purposeful: it allowed us to isolate the AI-handling logic within a dedicated
middleware component rather than embedding it directly into the AGP. An alternative approach could have involved a deeper integration with a single platform,
such as INGInious, which would have resulted in tighter coupling between system
components and potentially reduced flexibility. By keeping the middleware as an
independent layer, the system can easily accommodate additional AI models or
integrate with other AGPs in the future.

4.2

Middleware

The middleware serves as the central orchestration hub of the proposed system. It
acts as an intermediary layer, managing communication and data flow between the
AGP and various AI service providers, while also functioning as a persistence layer
for interaction data. In this architecture, the middleware functions as the central
component in the Mediator design pattern. The pattern’s intent, as defined by
Gamma et al. (1994), is to:
Define an object that encapsulates how a set of objects interact. Mediator promotes loose coupling by keeping objects from referring to each
other explicitly, and it lets you vary their interaction independently.
— Gamma et al. [28]
By channeling all communication between the AGP (a ’Colleague’ in the
pattern’s terminology) and the various AI models (other ’Colleagues’) through
this central middleware (the ’Mediator’), our design achieves this goal, effectively
decoupling the components and centralizing the interaction logic. Its primary role
is to abstract the complexities of AI integration, providing a unified interface for
requesting and receiving feedback.
Key responsibilities of the middleware include:
• API Management: Exposing well-defined API endpoints for receiving
submission data from the AGP and returning generated feedback. It handles
incoming requests and formats outgoing responses.
• Data Persistence: Serving as a persistence layer, storing relevant data from
interactions with both the AGP (e.g., student submissions, exercise context)
and the AI models (e.g., generated prompts, feedback received). This is
crucial for logging, analysis, tracking history, and potentially enforcing usage
policies (e.g., the number of feedback requests per student per exercise).
20

• AI Model Interaction: Managing connections and interactions with multiple, potentially heterogeneous AI models (e.g., OpenAI GPT, Anthropic
Claude, local models). This includes selecting the appropriate model based
on configuration or request parameters.
• Prompt Construction: Dynamically generating effective prompts for the
AI models using a flexible mechanism that incorporates submission details,
exercise context, instructor-defined guidelines, and other relevant factors
(detailed in Section 5.6).
• Policy Enforcement: Implementing and enforcing rules defined by instructors or administrators, such as limiting the number of feedback requests per
student per exercise, potentially content filtering, or other usage policies. This
is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the feedback process and ensuring
that it aligns with educational goals.
By centralizing these functions, the middleware simplifies the integration process
for the AGP and allows for consistent application of feedback policies across different
courses or exercises.

4.3

Client Interface

To simplify the process of connecting the AGP (or other educational tools) to the
middleware, the proposed system includes a dedicated client interface component.
This component is conceptualized as a Software Development Kit (SDK) or client
library designed specifically for interacting with the middleware’s Application
Programming Interface (API), which will be defined later in Section 5.3.
The primary goals of this client interface are:
• Abstraction: To hide the underlying complexities of network communication
with the middleware. This includes managing HTTP requests and responses,
handling errors, and ensuring data is formatted correctly for transmission.
• Ease of Use: To provide developers/instructors with a set of high-level,
intuitive functions or methods for performing common operations, such as
submitting exercise data or fetching the generated AI feedback.
• Simplified Integration: To significantly reduce the development effort
required to make an AGP communicate effectively with the middleware,
thereby promoting adoption and usability.

21

• Platform Adaptability: The design allows for the possibility of creating
specialized versions of the client interface tailored to the specific needs or
environment of different AGPs, further streamlining the integration process.
By providing this client interface, the system aims to lower the barrier for
integrating AI feedback capabilities into diverse educational technology ecosystems.
Developers can leverage this SDK rather than implementing the communication
protocol from scratch.

4.4

Conclusion

In this chapter, we have presented a high-level architecture that positions a dedicated
middleware layer as the mediator between an AGP and multiple AI service providers.
By centralizing communication and orchestration within this middleware, we achieve
loose coupling, improved modularity, and simplified management of heterogeneous
AI models. This design not only streamlines the integration process, allowing
different AGP environments and AI back-ends to be plugged in with minimal effort,
but also provides a unified persistence and policy-enforcement layer for recording
submissions, prompts, and feedback history.
We deliberately maintain an abstract integration interface rather than embedding AI-specific logic into any single AGP. This approach enhances maintainability,
promotes reuse across courses and distinct AGP, and future-proofs the system
against evolving AI capabilities. By preserving the autonomy of both the AGP and
the AI services, each component can be scaled, tested, and deployed independently.
The next chapter will dive into the detailed implementation of this architecture,
exploring the core modules, API endpoints, and prompt construction mechanisms
that bring this conceptual design to life.

5.1

Technology Stack

The selection of technologies for the middleware and its components was guided by
requirements for performance, scalability, ease of development, and compatibility,

document model. This flexibility is advantageous when handling diverse structures such as student submissions, exercise metadata, and AI-generated feedback,
which can vary significantly across exercises or evolve over time. MongoDB’s
presence in INGInious-related infrastructure also supports smoother deployment
and maintenance.
For deployment and environment consistency, Docker is used to containerize

The Flask-based middleware exposes a RESTful API to enable communication
with the AGP (typically via the client library). The primary endpoints manage
exercises, submissions, and feedback requests.

• POST /exercises: Initializes a new exercise instance for a specific student.
This endpoint is called once per student per exercise.
Request Body:
– ai_model (string): Identifier of the AI model, selected from the list
returned by /ais.
– question (string): The programming question or exercise statement.
– system_prompt (string, optional): Prompt to guide the AI’s behavior.
Defaults to a predefined system value.
– max_nb_of_feedbacks (int, optional): Maximum number of feedback
requests allowed.

– metadata (object): Optional metadata dictionary for additional context.

Request Body:
– student_input (string): The student’s code or solution.

• GET /exercises/<exercise_instance_id>

5.4

Database Schema

The middleware utilizes MongoDB to persist data related to exercises, student
submissions, and generated AI feedback. The choice of MongoDB, a documentoriented database, allows for a flexible structure that naturally maps to the system’s
data entities.

Document Structure
The database design centers around a main MongoDB collection where each document represents a unique instance of a student interacting with a specific exercise.
This top-level document contains metadata defining the exercise configuration for
27

that interaction (e.g., prompts, AI model choice, feedback policies) and embeds
an array holding all submissions made by that student for that specific exercise
instance. This structure is illustrated conceptually in Figure 5.2.
A key aspect of this design is that exercise configuration data (like prompts
and model selection) is stored within each student-exercise instance document.
Ideally, such configuration would be stored once per exercise. However, due to
integration constraints with platforms like INGInious, where the middleware is
typically contacted only upon a student’s submission action, there isn’t a natural
point to create or update a central exercise record independently. Therefore, the
exercise configuration is captured and stored when the first interaction for a student
on that exercise occurs. While this approach simplifies integration with the current
workflow, it leads to data redundancy, as the same exercise configuration details
are duplicated across multiple student records. Future enhancements could explore
alternative integration strategies, perhaps involving direct communication with the
middleware during exercise creation or updates on the platform, to enable a more
normalized data structure.

Figure 5.2: Conceptual document structure in MongoDB, showing an exercise
instance document containing an embedded array of submission documents.
Despite this limitation, the embedded design, where each student-exercise
interaction is stored in a single document, was chosen for several practical reasons.
In the context of educational platforms, it is common to access the entire history
of a student’s attempts or simply the most recent one when generating feedback.
Embedding all related submissions in a single document optimizes for this access
pattern by allowing the system to retrieve all relevant data in one query. This
approach also avoids the need for complex joins or multiple queries, simplifying
both the implementation and maintenance. Furthermore, it provides a logical and
28

self-contained representation of the one-to-many relationship between an exercise
instance and its submissions, aligning well with the current interaction model used
in the middleware.

Exercise Instance Fields
Each top-level document in the primary collection contains fields such as:
• id: A unique identifier for this specific student-exercise interaction record.
This corresponds to the identifier returned by the POST /exercises endpoint.
• ai_model: The identifier of the AI model configured for providing feedback
on this exercise (e.g., "gpt-4o", "gpt-4o-mini").
• system_prompt: The system prompt that sets the context and behavior for
the AI model, typically including pedagogical guidelines and constraints. See
Section 5.6 for details on how this is constructed.
• question: The text or description of the programming exercise/question.
• max_nb_of_feedbacks: An integer specifying the maximum number of AI
feedback requests allowed for this exercise. This field helps enforce limits
on feedback requests, ensuring fair usage, preventing excessive resource
consumption, and respecting educational objectives.
• metadata: A flexible sub-document (object) for storing additional contextual
information related to the exercise setup (e.g. course name, programming
language, ...).
• submissions: An array containing embedded submission documents (described below).

by the student and includes fields such as:
• id: A unique identifier for this specific submission attempt.
• timestamp: The date and time when the submission was received by the
middleware.
• student_input: The actual code or text submitted by the student.

time, error messages).
• feedback: A sub-document containing the AI-generated feedback, which

– response: The textual feedback generated by the AI model.

Adding New AI Models
The architecture is explicitly designed to facilitate the addition of new AI models
with minimal effort. The process typically involves the following steps:
1. Create a New Model Class: Implement a new Python class that inherits
from the AbstractAIModel base class.
2. Implement Core Methods: Override the required abstract methods, primarily the one responsible for sending the prompt(s) to the specific AI model’s
API and parsing its response. This involves handling API authentication,
request formatting, and response extraction specific to the new provider.
3. Register the Model: Make the new class discoverable by the AIManager,
typically by adding an entry to a configuration map.
Due to the abstraction provided by AbstractAIModel, the code required for
a new integration is often concise (potentially under 100 lines of Python code),
focusing solely on the communication specifics for that particular AI model’s API.
This promotes extensibility and allows the system to easily adapt as new AI models
become available.

5.6

Prompt System

The quality and pedagogical appropriateness of the AI-generated feedback heavily
depend on the prompts provided to the LLM. The middleware implements a flexible
33

prompt system designed to give instructors control over the AI’s behavior while
simplifying the process of incorporating dynamic context from the exercise and the
student’s submission.

Prompt Structure
The system primarily follows the common two-part prompt structure supported by
many conversational LLMs: the system prompt and the user prompt.
The system prompt defines the overall context, persona, and constraints for
the AI model. It typically specifies the exercise objectives, the expected tone and
style of feedback (such as acting as a helpful teaching assistant without revealing full
solutions), and any pedagogical guidelines. It may also indicate the programming
language or specific concepts being assessed. This prompt is usually static for
a given exercise configuration, providing a consistent foundation for all student
interactions. An example used in this project is shown in Listing 6.1.
In contrast, the user prompt captures the immediate context of a specific
feedback request. It usually includes the student’s submitted code, any relevant
error messages or auto-grader output, and a framing question such as, "Can you
help me understand why my code fails this test case?" This dynamic part of the
prompt varies with each submission and allows the system to tailor its response
to the individual student’s attempt. An example of a user prompt template is
presented in Listing 6.2.
This separation between the system and user prompts enables the model to
maintain consistent behavior across submissions while still adapting its feedback to
each specific context.

Placeholder Mechanism
To make prompts dynamic and context-aware, both system and user prompt
templates can contain placeholders that the middleware replaces with actual data
before sending the request to the AI model. A simple templating syntax (e.g., using
curly braces) is employed.
Common placeholders include:
• In the System Prompt:
– {question}: Replaced with the text description of the exercise.
– {<key>}: Allows access to custom metadata fields defined for the exercise.
• In the User Prompt:
34

– {student_input}: Replaced with the student’s submitted code.
– {<key>}: Allows access to custom metadata fields associated with the
submission.

Default and Custom Prompts
The system supports both default prompts and the ability for instructors to provide
custom prompts for each exercise:
• Default Prompts: The middleware includes built-in default system and
user prompt templates designed based on general pedagogical principles, such
as encouraging critical thinking, providing hints rather than direct answers,
focusing on conceptual understanding, and maintaining a supportive tone.
These serve as a baseline for providing feedback if no custom prompts are
specified. The specific content of these default prompts used in our use case
validation is detailed in Listings 6.1 and 6.2.
• Custom Prompts: Instructors can override the default prompts by specifying custom system prompt text (and potentially a custom user prompt
template structure) when configuring an exercise. This allows tailoring the
AI’s feedback style, focus, and constraints to the specific learning objectives,
difficulty level, and context of a particular exercise or course. The custom
prompts are stored in the exercise instance document in the database (field
system_prompt).
The flexibility of the prompt system is a key feature, allowing for both rapid
deployment with sensible defaults and the ability to customize the AI’s behavior
to align with specific educational goals or pedagogical strategies.

5.7

Python Client Library

To facilitate interaction between AGPs and the middleware API, as conceptually outlined in Section 4.3, a dedicated Python client library, named AICourseAssistant,
was developed. This library abstracts the underlying HTTP requests and provides
a more convenient programmatic interface for developers/instructors looking to
integrate AI feedback into their systems.
The AICourseAssistant class serves as the primary client-side interface for
communicating with the middleware API. Its key functionalities include:
• Connection Setup: The server() class method initializes the library
by specifying the middleware’s base URL and an optional access token for
35

authentication. A check (_check_server()) ensures this setup is done before
other operations.
• Instance Management: Each AICourseAssistant instance represents a
specific interaction between a student and an exercise. There are two distinct
initialization paths, and it is the responsibility of the AGP (e.g., INGInious)
to determine which one applies based on the context of the interaction:
– New Interaction: When a student starts an exercise for the first time,
a new instance should be created using the class constructor __init__(),
which takes a BaseDataModel object containing the full configuration
(AI model, question, prompts, etc.). This process also sends an initial
submission to the middleware and creates a new record in the backend.
– Existing Interaction (e.g., Resubmission): If a student is resubmitting an answer to an exercise they have already attempted, the
platform should re-use the original exercise instance. This is done via
the from_data_id() class method, which takes the previously assigned
exercise_instance_id. This links the new submission to the same
exercise context in the middleware database and avoids duplicating
configuration data.
• Configuration: Various setter methods are provided (e.g., set_ai_model(),
set_question(), set_max_nb_of_feedbacks(), set_system_prompt(), set_
metadata()) to define the parameters for a new exercise instance. Similarly, methods like set_submission_data(), set_student_input(), set_
student_prompt(), and set_submission_metadata() are used to prepare
the details of a specific student submission attempt.
• Communication: The central send() method orchestrates the communication with the middleware. Its behavior depends on how the AICourseAssistant
instance was initialized. If the instance represents a new student-exercise
interaction (typically created via the constructor __init__), send() uses
the internal _send_data() method to transmit the complete exercise configuration and the first submission details via a POST /exercises request.
Conversely, if the instance was initialized for an existing interaction using
from_data_id(), indicating a subsequent submission, send() uses the internal _send_submission() method to send only the new submission data via
a POST /exercises/<exercise_instance_id>/submissions request, referencing the known exercise instance ID. In both cases, the method handles
the underlying HTTP request, manages timeouts and potential exceptions,
and parses the JSON response from the middleware to store the relevant
identifiers (exercise_instance_id, submission_id) internally.
36

• Feedback Access: The feedback_url() method constructs the specific API
endpoint URL required by the frontend component to fetch feedback status or
content asynchronously (GET /exercises/<id>/submissions/<sub_id>).
Overall, the AICourseAssistant Python library provides a crucial abstraction
layer, significantly simplifying the process of integrating the AI feedback middleware
into educational platforms like INGInious by handling API communication, state
management, and user interface generation details.

5.8

Scalability and Dockerization

Given that interactions with LLMs can sometimes involve significant latency, and
that an AGP might serve numerous students concurrently, the scalability of the
middleware is an important consideration. The implementation addresses this
primarily through containerization and a stateless design.
• Dockerization: As mentioned in Section 5.1, the entire middleware application (Flask server, dependencies) is packaged into a Docker container. This
provides several benefits:
– Consistency: Ensures the application runs identically regardless of the
host environment.
– Isolation: Dependencies are contained within the image, avoiding
conflicts.
– Simplified Deployment: Standardized unit of deployment managed
by container orchestration tools.
• Docker Compose: For development and smaller-scale deployments, Docker
Compose is used to define and run the multi-container application, typically
including the middleware container and a MongoDB container. This simplifies
the setup process.
• Stateless Middleware Design: The Flask application itself is designed to
be stateless. This means that it does not store any session-specific data in its
own memory between requests. All persistent state (exercise configurations,
submission history, feedback) is stored externally in the MongoDB database.
• Horizontal Scalability: The combination of containerization and a stateless design enables straightforward horizontal scaling. If the load increases,
multiple instances of the middleware container can be run behind a load
balancer. Since each instance is stateless and relies on the central database,
37

any instance can handle any incoming request. This allows the system to
handle a larger volume of concurrent users and potentially long-running AI
feedback requests without blocking other users.
By leveraging Docker and adhering to a stateless architecture, the middleware
is prepared for deployment scenarios requiring high availability and the ability to
handle fluctuating loads, which are common in educational platform contexts.

Validation
The goal of this chapter is to assess the effectiveness of the AI-based feedback
system integrated into the INGInious platform, as introduced in the previous
chapters. The validation focuses on evaluating whether the tool helps students
improve their programming solutions and supports their learning process.
To achieve this, we conducted a twofold validation approach. First, we performed
a quantitative analysis using data collected from student submissions and AI
interaction logs. This analysis involved computing a set of metrics designed to
measure the impact of AI feedback on students’ success rates, number of attempts,
and progression patterns.
Second, we administered anonymous surveys to students at two different stages
of the deployment. These surveys aimed to gather qualitative insights into how
students perceived the feedback generated by the AI assistant, how they used it,
and whether they found it useful or potentially misleading.
By combining both quantitative and qualitative evidence, this validation seeks
to determine to what extent the AI-powered feedback system contributes to student
learning and whether it meets the objectives stated at the beginning of this work.

7.1

Method

To evaluate the effectiveness of the AI feedback system, we adopted a dual methodology combining quantitative data analysis and qualitative user feedback. This
section outlines the procedures followed for both approaches.

Quantitative Methodology
The quantitative analysis was based on data retrieved from a combination of the
INGInious submission database and the internal database of the AI feedback tool.
48

By correlating entries from both sources, we were able to generate a comprehensive
dataset capturing student activity and AI interactions.
Two datasets were constructed for this purpose:
• Cohort 1: Data from the initial large-scale deployment of the tool.
• Cohort 2: Data from a smaller, later deployment intended to validate
improvements.
Custom scripts were used to process and correlate the data, extracting both
global and per-exercise statistics. To ensure a more meaningful analysis of the
feedback system’s effectiveness, we only considered submissions until the first
successful attempt for each student on each exercise. This approach helps isolate
the impact of feedback on the initial learning process. Global metrics included the
total number of submissions, number of unique students, and the number of AI
feedback requests. More advanced indicators were also computed, such as:
• success rate after feedback: the proportion of feedback requests followed
immediately by a successful submission;
• success rate after failure: the proportion of failed submissions without feedback
that were followed by success;
• feedback improvement: the difference between these two rates, quantifying
the added value of AI feedback;
In addition, per-exercise statistics helped highlight variations in feedback usage
and success rates across different problem types.

Qualitative Methodology
To complement the numerical analysis, two anonymous surveys were conducted,
one for each validation phase. Anonymity was crucial to encourage honest and
unbiased responses. However, it also meant we could not link individual survey
answers back to each student’s submission record, preventing us from correlating
subjective perceptions with actual performance data.
The surveys included both closed and open-ended questions designed to assess
students’ prior programming experience, use of external AI tools, frequency and
usefulness of the integrated feedback, clarity and relevance of the messages, and
possible drawbacks such as confusion or over-reliance. Students were also invited
to provide suggestions for improving the tool and its user interface.
These qualitative insights helped contextualize the quantitative findings and
provided a deeper understanding of how students perceived and engaged with the
AI assistant.

7.3

Analysis and Discussion

Student Background and AI Familiarity
Most respondents in both surveys classified themselves as beginners, which is
expected for a first-year programming cohort. This novice baseline likely increases
reliance on any hinting system and may amplify both the benefits and the confusions
introduced by AI feedback.

General AI Usage Trends
Between December 2024 and April/May 2025, nearly all students reported using
external AI tools (e.g. ChatGPT, Copilot) for assistance with programming exercises, often copying generated code without full comprehension. This rising trend
underscores both the demand for on-demand code help and the risk of superficial
learning when uncontrolled tools are used.
53

Uptake of the Integrated Feedback Tool
Students invoked the in-platform feedback on average 1–2 times per exercise when
they did not succeed on the first attempt. Would they have bypassed our service
and gone straight to ChatGPT had we not provided an integrated assistant? Given
the high frequency of AI use for programming assistance and the fact that 75–82 %
of respondents believe our tool will curb external AI reliance, the evidence suggests
they would have. This indicates that our integrated assistant is not only a valuable
resource but also a potential deterrent against unmoderated AI use.

Quantitative Effectiveness
Cohort 1 experienced a +5.02 pp lift in success rate when feedback was used
(25.00 % vs. 19.98 %), while Cohort 2 saw an even larger +15.10 pp gain (27.85
% vs. 12.75 %). These differentials demonstrate that students convert AI hints into
correct submissions more often than they recover on their own.
It is important to note that these figures may be slightly understated: students
frequently correct trivial mistakes (e.g. syntax errors) without requesting AI feedback, so the measured “self-recovery” rate includes many low-effort fixes. As a
result, the true benefit of AI feedback for more substantive misunderstandings is
likely even greater than these numbers suggest.
To determine whether these differences are statistically significant, we performed
a Two-Proportion Z-Test [38]. This test evaluates whether the observed increase in
immediate success rate after feedback could be due to chance, or if it reflects a real
improvement attributable to the AI tool.
We used a two-tailed version of the test, as we sought to verify whether the
success rate differs significantly in either direction—positive or negative—depending
on feedback use. While our hypothesis was directional (expecting an increase),
the two-tailed approach ensures a more conservative and rigorous validation of the
observed differences.
For each cohort, we compared the proportion of immediate successes after
requesting feedback against the proportion of immediate successes after a failed
attempt without requesting feedback.
The results are as follows:
• Cohort 1: The success rate after feedback was 25.00% (241 out of 964),
compared to 19.98% (333 out of 1667) without feedback. The Z-test returned
a p-value of 0.0026, indicating a statistically significant difference at the 0.01
level.
• Cohort 2: The success rate after feedback was 27.85% (22 out of 79),
compared to 12.75% (38 out of 298) without feedback. The Z-test yielded a
54

p-value of 0.0011, also statistically significant.
These results confirm that the observed improvements in both cohorts are
unlikely to be due to random variation. Full details of the calculations are provided
in Appendix E.

Perceived Helpfulness and Added Complexity
Strength of Perceived Value
Nearly 90% of students rated the feedback 3 or above on this 1–5 scale, and over
half (55%) rated it 4 or 5, demonstrating that most users found the hints more than
moderately helpful. Virtually all respondents agreed that the integrated assistant
is an educational asset, with 94.4% in Survey 1 and 100% in Survey 2 affirming its
value.
Not All Feedback “Lands”
Although most feedback was helpful, between one-third and one-half of respondents
occasionally encountered incorrect or irrelevant suggestions; around 20 % reported
that feedback sometimes made the exercise harder; and about half of the students
stated they were occasionally diverted from their own problem-solving approach.
Discussion: The drop in incorrect or irrelevant feedback from 55 % in Cohort
1 to 33 % in Cohort 2 suggests that refined prompt engineering and model tuning
can substantially reduce low-value hints. The 20 % incidence of increased difficulty
appears acceptable given that the assistant is optional, students who find it detrimental can simply opt out, providing a natural self-regulation mechanism. Likewise,
divergence from a student’s chosen problem-solving path is not universally negative:
when clearly labeled as a “hint” rather than a solution, steering learners away from
unproductive strategies may actually enhance their understanding.

Shifting Behavior Toward Controlled AI Use
Crucially, 75–82 % of students believe that using our integrated tool will reduce
their reliance on external AI services, aligning with our objective of guiding learners
toward a controlled, curriculum-aware feedback system rather than unmoderated
code generators.

Evolution from v1 to v2
Between the first and second deployments, feedback improvement rose from +5.02
pp to +15.10 pp, and the share of students finding the level of detail “just right”
55

increased from 73 % to 89 %. Concurrently, reported incorrect or irrelevant feedback
fell from 55 % to 33 %, perceptions of “over-helping” dropped from 15 % to 11
%, agreement that the tool is an educational asset rose from 95 % to 100 %, and
expectation of reduced external AI use climbed from 75 % to 82 %. These gains
strongly suggest that targeted prompt refinements and model updates directly
enhance both the objective effectiveness and subjective satisfaction of AI-generated
feedback.

7.4

Threats to Validity

In this section, we reflect on factors that may have influenced our quantitative and
qualitative findings, and that could limit the reproducibility or generalisability of
the results.
Sample Size and Cohort Composition Both validation rounds relied on
relatively small cohorts (272 students in Cohort 1 and only 25 in Cohort 2), with
survey participation even lower (90 and 11 respondents, respectively). Such modest
sample sizes reduce statistical power, amplify the impact of outliers, and hinder
meaningful subgroup analyses (e.g., by prior experience or by exercise type). In
particular, the tiny size of the second cohort precludes any robust comparison
between AI models, prompt variants, or interface tweaks, leaving open questions
about which specific design changes drove the observed improvements.
Voluntary Participation and Selection Bias Use of the integrated feedback
tool, and the decision to complete the associated surveys, was entirely voluntary.
This self-selection may have skewed the data toward students who are already
more motivated, more comfortable with technology, or who struggle and thus seek
extra help. Although our surveys captured a snapshot of respondents’ backgrounds,
these cannot fully characterise the non-respondents, making it difficult to ascertain
whether the behaviors and perceptions we measured are representative of the wider
student population.
Limited Exercise Coverage The exercise sets used in each deployment (4 in the
first phase, 9 in the second) sample only a subset of first-year programming topics.
As a result, our findings may not extend to other problem categories, particularly
those involving more complex algorithms, data structures, or collaborative projects.
In practice, different exercise formats or pedagogical contexts could yield different
patterns of AI usage and efficacy.

56

Self-Reported Survey Data All qualitative insights stem from anonymous,
self-reported responses, which are subject to biases such as social desirability, recall
errors, and misinterpretation of questions. While we designed the surveys to be as
clear and neutral as possible, students may have interpreted questions differently or
provided answers that they believed were expected rather than their true opinions.
This could lead to over- or under-reporting of certain behaviors or perceptions.
Taken together, these threats underscore the exploratory nature of our validation.
While the results are promising, further studies with larger, more diverse cohorts,
and a broader exercise portfolio will be needed to confirm and generalise our
conclusions.

7.5

Conclusion

In this chapter, we have presented a two-pronged validation of our AI-powered
feedback system within the INGInious platform. The quantitative analysis, conducted on two cohorts, demonstrated that students who requested AI hints were
consistently more likely to convert a failing submission into a correct one, yielding
an average lift of +5.02 percentage points in the first deployment and +15.10
percentage points in the second.
Complementing these figures, our anonymous surveys revealed that the vast
majority of respondents found the integrated feedback clear, appropriately detailed,
and conducive to their learning. Although a non-negligible fraction reported
occasional irrelevant suggestions or mild disruption of their own problem-solving
strategies, these issues diminished substantially between the first and second rounds,
indicating that iterative prompt and model refinements can markedly improve
feedback quality.
We also discussed several limitations, most notably small sample sizes, voluntary participation bias, and restricted exercise coverage, which caution against
overgeneralizing our findings. Nonetheless, the consistent positive trends across
both objective metrics and student perceptions strongly suggest that an in-platform
AI assistant can effectively support novice programmers, while steering them away
from unmoderated external tools.
Taken together, these results validate the core hypothesis of this work: a
curriculum-aware, integrated AI feedback system can enhance student success and
engagement in early programming courses. Future studies with larger cohorts,
broader exercise sets, and controlled experimental designs will be essential to
consolidate and extend these promising outcomes.

Chapter 8
Conclusion
This thesis began by identifying a critical gap in existing Automated Grading
Platforms (AGPs), which typically struggle to offer the kind of detailed, adaptive
feedback that human graders provide. Students often bypass built-in or even
human guidance by turning to external AI tools, undermining formative learning
and academic integrity. Motivated by this challenge, this work set out to design a
middleware layer that could integrate AI-generated, pedagogically aligned feedback
directly into AGPs, aiming at curbing uncontrolled off-the-shelf AI usage while
preserving course objectives.
To meet these goals, a flexible, provider-agnostic middleware architecture was
proposed in Chapter 4. By centralizing communication and orchestration, the
design achieves loose coupling, improved modularity and simplified management of
heterogeneous AI models. An abstract integration interface ensures the middleware
remains independent of any single grading environment, promoting maintainability
and reuse across courses and platforms.
Chapter 5 detailed the concrete implementation using Flask, MongoDB and
Docker. The system exposes RESTful endpoints for submission handling, employs
a MongoDB schema centered on exercise instances with embedded submissions,
and uses an extensible AI model management layer built on an abstract base class.
A flexible prompt system with placeholder support adapts feedback to exercise
context. The AICourseAssistant Python client library streamlines adoption by
educators and developers.
In Chapter 6, we explored the practical deployment of this middleware within
programming exercises. Careful selection of AI models and iterative refinement of
prompt templates demonstrated how tailored feedback can reinforce core concepts.
Crucially, the validation in Chapter 7 demonstrates that this integrated AI
assistant delivers measurable learning gains and high user satisfaction. In two
deployment phases within the INGInious platform, students who requested AI
hints converted failing attempts into correct submissions at rates 25.00 % versus
58

19.98 % in Cohort 1 and 27.85 % versus 12.75 % in Cohort 2, corresponding to
lifts of +5.02 and +15.10 percentage points respectively. Two-Proportion Z-Tests
confirmed these improvements are statistically significant.
Complementing these quantitative gains, anonymous surveys revealed that 85
% of respondents in the first cohort and 89 % in the second cohort rated feedback
usefulness at least "moderate", while over half assigned scores of 4 or 5 on a 5-point
scale. The share of students encountering incorrect or irrelevant suggestions fell
from 55.2 % to 33.3 % after iterative prompt refinements, and those judging the
level of detail "just right" rose from 73.1 % to 89 %. Moreover, 75 % to 82 % of
participants indicated that using the in-platform tool would reduce their reliance
on uncontrolled external AI services. These converging trends provide evidence
that a curriculum-aware, integrated AI feedback system might both enhance novice
programmers’ success and steer them toward a controlled learning environment.
Several limitations temper these findings however. Cohort sizes were modest
(272 and 25 students respectively), survey participation was voluntary and exercise
coverage remained limited, which may introduce selection bias and restrict generalizability. LLMs may still hallucinate or provide irrelevant suggestions, and the
system’s dependence on prompt engineering and model selection requires ongoing
effort to maintain quality.
In summary, this work meets its initial objectives by delivering a scalable,
modular middleware prototype (Chapter 4), a full reference implementation (Chapter 5), practical application studies (Chapter 6) and rigorous validation (Chapter 7).
The demonstrated lifts in success rates, positive student perceptions and clear
path for iterative improvement establish a solid foundation for future research
and deployment in authentic educational settings, even though some remaining
limitations still need to be overcome.

Chapter 9
Future Work
The rapid evolution of AI models and techniques opens numerous opportunities to
extend the contributions of this thesis. The following directions highlight promising
paths for further research and development.

Comparison of AI Models
The middleware architecture developed in this thesis can readily accommodate a
broad spectrum of language models. A natural next step is to perform a systematic
comparison between the standard models used here (for example GPT-4o, Gemini
2.0, etc) and emerging reasoning-focused models such as the OpenAI O-series or
DeepSeek R1, as well as coding-specialized models like GPT-4.1 and Claude 3.7
Sonnet (see Section 2.2 of Chapter 2). Such an evaluation would involve defining
a common set of programming exercises, instrumenting the middleware to record
detailed feedback metrics (accuracy, relevance, depth) and conducting controlled
deployments with real student cohorts. The goal would be to identify which models
deliver the most effective hints for different types of programming tasks, and how
these models compare in terms of computational efficiency and cost. This analysis
could also explore the impact of model size and architecture on feedback quality,
providing valuable insights for educators selecting AI tools.

Prompt Engineering Refinement
While this thesis demonstrated the benefits of refined prompt templates, it only
scratched the surface of advanced prompt engineering techniques. Future work
should explore few-shot prompting, chain-of-thought prompting and other strategies
to improve model performance (see Section 2.3 of Chapter 2). Real-world case
studies can compare different prompt variants head-to-head, measuring not only
60

objective improvements in submission success but also student perceptions of
clarity and usefulness. Building an automated prompt evaluation framework, where
feedback performance data is continuously collected and used to refine templates,
would allow the system to evolve in response to actual learning outcomes.

Conversational Feedback Loops
The current system delivers one-shot feedback, giving students a single, selfcontained response for each submission. A natural extension is to explore multi-turn
dialogue, allowing learners to ask follow-up questions or request clarifications in
real time. Future research could examine whether such conversational interactions
improve error-correction rates, reduce time-to-fix, or enhance learner motivation
compared with one-shot feedback.

Scalability, Cost, and Environmental Impact
Large-scale adoption raises practical questions about latency, financial cost, and
ecological footprint. Systematic studies that measure performance under heavy
classroom loads, estimate total operating expenses, and quantify energy consumption would clarify the trade-offs between richer feedback and sustainable resource
use. Insights from this work could guide institutions seeking to support very large
cohorts without compromising affordability or environmental goals.

Instructor Adoption and Workflow Integration
The success of AI-assisted grading also depends on how easily educators can
incorporate it into their routines. Future investigations should focus on teacher
perceptions of usability, trust, and workload impact, assessing whether the tools
genuinely simplify marking or introduce new overhead. Mixed-methods studies,
combining usage data with surveys or interviews, can reveal barriers to adoption
and inform the design of educator-centric features that align with established
assessment practices.

Deep Integration into a Specific AGP
Embedding the middleware natively into a target platform such as INGInious
would eliminate data duplication, reduce end-to-end latency and leverage existing

61

exercise metadata and submission histories. A close integration could expose inplatform customization interfaces so that instructors adjust prompt templates and
model-selection policies without modifying code. In addition, real-time analytics
dashboards could correlate grading engine logs with AI interaction traces, enabling
educators to monitor common misconceptions and feedback usage in one unified
environment.

Extension to Non-Coding Domains
The provider-agnostic design of the middleware makes it amenable to domains
beyond programming. By refining system prompts, the same architecture could
deliver automated feedback for mathematics exercises, written-language assignments or laboratory reports. Cross-disciplinary validation studies would assess the
generality of AI-powered feedback, uncover domain adaptation requirements and
broaden the educational impact of the approach described in Chapter 4.

Domain-Specific Model Fine-Tuning
Curating discipline-specific corpora (open-source code repositories, mathematical
proof libraries or specialized textbooks) and fine-tuning base LLMs on these datasets
could yield models with superior technical accuracy. Future experiments should
compare off-the-shelf models against fine-tuned variants in terms of error diagnosis
precision, hint usefulness and overall student learning gains. This would also explore
the trade-offs between general-purpose models and domain-specific adaptations,
providing guidance on when fine-tuning is beneficial.

IDE-Based Pedagogical Assistant
Integrating the middleware into editors such as Visual Studio Code or JetBrains
IDEs would place formative feedback where learners actually write code, yet
without adopting the auto-coding behaviour of plugins like GitHub Copilot. Copilot
often delivers complete snippets, which can short-circuit understanding and create
academic-integrity risks; the assistant outlined here instead serves as a scaffolded
tutor. By examining the exercise specification, the student’s current code and any
compiler diagnostics, it supplies step-by-step hints and conceptual explanations that
encourage self-correction while never disclosing a full solution. The amount and
style of assistance remain adjustable through the middleware, allowing instructors
to set suitable hint granularity for each assignment. In this way, AI guidance is

62

woven into the student workflow while preserving the reflective practice essential
for learning programming.
As AI research advances, new models and techniques will continue to emerge.
The modular, provider-agnostic architecture presented in this thesis ensures that
future innovations can be incorporated with minimal effort, driving ever richer and
more effective pedagogical feedback.
